Adjusted: every city's figure is divided by its metro area's price level (BEA regional price parities, US average = 100), so the numbers show what each city spends at the same prices. Adjusting lowers the figures of expensive metros and raises those of cheaper ones, so how much it changes San Diego's gap depends on the peers too. It is a rough correction: prices for households are not the same as the wages and construction costs a city pays.

The peers are 11 US cities of roughly 0.6 to 1.7 million people, chosen to be broadly similar and to cover several regions. Two much smaller cities, Arlington County VA and Vancouver WA, are shown as hollow rings for reference only: they do not count toward the middle peer, the range or the rank. (Arlington is a county government, so its "health, welfare, jails & schools" line is very large; Vancouver reports $0 for libraries, which a separate district runs.) A higher number is not waste: it can reflect higher prices or wages, or a different mix of services. Austin's figures are mostly Census estimates rather than its own reports in most years. Fiscal years end in different months. By default nothing is adjusted for cost of living; tick the box below to put every city on the same price level. This compares San Diego with other cities, not with the private sector. "Administration, courts & inspection" is a broad Census category that includes spending not classified elsewhere, so it is the least comparable row: part of any gap there may reflect how cities report.
