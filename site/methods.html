Data & methods

Every number on this site comes from public files committed to the project's repository (data/raw/, with the source URL, retrieval date and checksum of each file in data/raw/MANIFEST.csv) and is rebuilt by scripts in scripts/.

Sources

  • Operating actuals (FY2011-23) and operating budget (FY2011-26): City of San Diego Department of Finance, data.sandiego.gov. Account and department reference tables come from the same portal.
  • Population: City of San Diego estimated population as reported in the California State Controller's Office local-government finance data (bythenumbers.sco.ca.gov).

How the numbers are built

  • Spending is every row whose account number starts with 5 (expense accounts). Revenue rows (starting with 4) are not used on this page.
  • Fiscal years are named by the year they end: FY2023 is July 2022 to June 2023.
  • Dollars are nominal, not adjusted for inflation.
  • Adopted budget only. The budget file also contains "proposed" rows for FY2024 and FY2025; they are excluded because adding them to the adopted rows would count the budget twice.
  • FY2026 budget rows have no budget stage in the source file. They are treated as the adopted budget. The City's open-data actuals file currently stops at FY2023.
  • Transfers Out (money moved between city funds) is about 13% of FY2023 spending. It is hidden by default because it counts the same dollars twice; the checkbox adds it back.
  • Credits. A department and spending type that nets to a credit cannot be drawn in a treemap. It is left out of the picture, included in the totals, and listed under the chart.
  • Unclassified. Rows whose department or account is missing from the City's reference tables are kept and labelled "Unclassified". Rows with a blank account number cannot be identified as spending or revenue and are excluded; their totals are recorded in data/processed/build_report.json.
  • Population is missing from the source for a few years. Those years are interpolated, and years after the latest report use the latest value. Per-resident figures for them are marked "(population estimated)".

Who gets paid (vendor payments)

  • Source: the City's yearly "Cumulative Amounts Paid to Vendors - $25,000 or more" reports (PDF), FY2022 to FY2026, read with pdftotext. The FY2026 report covers July 2025 to June 2026.
  • Only payments of $25,000 or more to a vendor in the year are published, so the totals leave out smaller vendors. They are not directly comparable to the City's spending either: spending includes payroll, while the vendor list includes capital, pass-through and other payments. The page shows the City's figure only for scale and says so.
  • No purpose or category is published, so a large payee can be a pass-through (for example to the pension system or a regional water wholesaler). The page makes no judgement about whether a payment was too high.
  • One company, several IDs or spellings. Vendors whose names match ignoring case, punctuation, "&" vs "and" and endings like Inc, LLC, Co or "The" are combined, as is a vendor ID renamed to another name starting with the same word. All vendor IDs are kept in data/processed/vendor_payments.csv. Two different companies with near-identical names could be combined by mistake.
  • Checks: the extractor fails on any line it does not recognise, page footers that are not exactly 1 to N, a page count that differs from the PDF's real page count, a duplicate vendor ID or an amount under $25,000. The reports print no totals, so the build also requires the number of vendors not to jump more than 35% year over year and each year's vendor payments to be between 40% and 110% of the City's own spending. The last check is a coarse sanity band (it mainly catches a lost first page or a double count); the page-count checks are what guard against a lost page.

Compared with other cities

  • Source: Census Bureau, Annual Survey of State and Local Government Finances, individual unit files for survey years 2019 to 2024 (census.gov), retrieved 2026-10-31. The 2022 survey was the five-yearly census of all governments. Amounts are in thousands of dollars in the source; the processed table stores dollars.
  • Peers: Phoenix, San Jose, San Antonio, Dallas, Austin, Fort Worth, Charlotte, Columbus, Seattle, Portland and Boston: US cities of roughly 0.6 to 1.7 million people, spread across regions. Seattle, Portland and Boston (0.64 to 0.75 million) are smaller than San Diego (1.38 million) and were added for regional spread. Consolidated city-counties (Indianapolis, Jacksonville, Philadelphia, San Francisco) are left out because they also provide county services. The list is in config/peers.json.
  • Reference cities: Arlington County, VA and Vancouver, WA (0.19 to 0.23 million people) are included for comparison with places of personal interest. They are built exactly like the peers but marked reference in config/peers.json and left out of the middle peer, range, rank and trend, so the median still describes cities of San Diego's size. Arlington is a county government (it provides county services and its schools are funded through it), so only its city-type lines are comparable. Their price levels are the Washington DC area and Portland-Vancouver metros.
  • Services: each service is current-operation plus construction spending under the Census item codes listed in config/census_functions.json. The build fails if a spending code is not mapped. Utilities, transit and airports, "health, welfare, jails and schools", interest and grants are separate groups, off by default, because cities differ in whether they provide them. San Diego reports a small amount in the health and welfare group (about $94 per resident); Boston's city government runs its schools, so its figure there is much larger.
  • $0 means "not reported": usually a county or special district provides the service. Such cities are left out of the middle peer and the rank.
  • Some figures are Census estimates, not reports. Austin did not return the survey in most years: nearly all its lines in 2019 to 2022 and 2024 are flagged by the Census as imputed (estimated). Treat Austin's numbers as approximate. A few lines for other cities are flagged the same way.
  • Administration is a catch-all. It includes the Census's "general, not elsewhere classified" spending, so differences there may partly reflect how each city reports rather than how much it spends.
  • Fiscal-year timing: a survey year covers each government's fiscal year that ended between July of the year before and June of that year. In the 2024 survey San Diego's ended June 2024, but Dallas, San Antonio, Austin and Fort Worth ended September 2023 and Columbus and Seattle ended December 2023, up to nine months earlier. Nothing is adjusted for that timing.
  • Price adjustment (optional): with "Adjust for local prices" on, each city's per-resident figure is divided by its metro area's price level, the BEA Regional Price Parity for all items (US average = 100; apps.bea.gov, table MARPP, retrieved 2026-10-31). A city's fiscal year spans two calendar years, so the level is the two years blended by months (a year ended June 2024 is half 2023 and half 2024). Dallas and Fort Worth share one metro area; every other city has its own. The metro for each city is in config/peers.json. This is a rough correction: the index measures what households pay for goods and rent, not the wages and construction costs a city pays. It is off by default and the as-reported and adjusted gaps are both shown in the trend table.
  • Over time: the trend compares San Diego with the middle peer in each survey year, in dollars of the day (no inflation adjustment), so the gap is meaningful but the amounts are not comparable across years. To keep the years comparable, "other capital outlay" (equipment and land), which the 2019 to 2021 files list apart from construction and later files fold into it, is counted with construction in every year, and intergovernmental transfers, which older files list by service, are grouped into "Grants to other governments". Many cities' broad administration line jumps in the 2022 survey, so the single-service trends are steadier. Census population estimates change between years, and some cities carry the same estimate across two surveys.
  • Peer gap, not margin: peers share the same pressures and habits, so this says nothing about what a private-sector provider would charge.

Homelessness

  • Sources: the California Interagency Council on Homelessness's system performance measures (data.ca.gov, through March 2026); the Department of Housing and Community Development's HHAP allocation tables, rounds 1 to 5; the Homelessness Strategies & Solutions Department pages of the City's adopted budgets for FY2023 to FY2026; and HUD's "2007-2024 Point-in-Time Estimates by CoC". HUD blocks automated downloads, so that workbook was downloaded in a browser and added by hand; the others are fetched by scripts/fetch_homelessness.py.
  • City dollars are that one department's spending, so they are a floor: police, libraries and other departments also spend on homelessness, and the State Auditor found in 2024 that the City could not total all of it. The department changed shape over these years (FY2021 includes one-off pandemic funds), and its revenue (mostly charges for services and transfers) is shown beside its spending and never netted off. The count it is divided by covers the city and county, so the per-person figure understates the city's spending per person counted in the city. Fiscal year N is set beside the January N count.
  • State dollars are HHAP awards, not spending, and HHAP is only one state program. The state sets aside separate amounts for the regional body, the city and the county; the page adds them for each area. The City's department revenue is mostly charges for services and transfers (such as hotel-tax money), and the budget does not say which funds paid for what, so city and state dollars are never added together. The "redirected" Round 1 table is used for round 1. Each award is divided by the count it was allocated on: 2019, 2022 or 2023, or 2017 for areas the tables mark as approved to use that older count (San Diego, rounds 1 to 3).
  • Counts are the one-night January counts for each Continuum of Care (a region that is usually a city plus its county), so they are not city populations and the page does not divide by population. 2021 is blank because the unsheltered count was waived. In some years HUD's file marks a region as "sheltered-only" but still lists an unsheltered figure; that figure is the region's last unsheltered count reused, so those totals are kept and flagged on the page.
  • Outcomes are the state's own measures (people served, exits to permanent housing, days homeless, returns within six months), published for California regions only. They are what the regions report and cannot show that any spending caused them. Exit rate is exits divided by people served.

Who receives federal dollars (by age)

A rough national estimate for fiscal year 2024 of how federal spending on people falls across age brackets. The brackets (0–18, 19–44, 45–64, 65–84, 85 and over) are the age groups CMS publishes health spending in, the coarsest source; generations (Silent, Baby Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z, Gen Alpha, by Pew's birth years) are labels only, and no dollar figure is divided between generations.

  • Totals come from the budget where a budget line exists, so those programs tie to their outlay (SNAP, school meals, WIC, HUD housing, SSI and the tax credits use their own agency or IRS figures, noted below): Treasury Monthly Treasury Statement (Social Security, Medicare), OMB historical table 3.2 (veterans, elementary and secondary education, higher education), and CMS national health expenditure table 5-3 for the federal portion of Medicaid payments (calendar 2024).
  • Social Security is split with the SSA Annual Statistical Supplement 2025 (December 2024 benefits by single year of age for retired workers, disabled workers and children), then scaled to the budget total. Spouse and survivor benefits only come in three age groups and are spread over those ages by population.
  • Medicare and Medicaid use CMS's age shape of spending, which is from 2020, the latest published; it is applied to 2024 dollars. Medicaid is the federal portion of payments, but the age shape covers federal plus state spending: the federal match is higher for expansion adults (about 90%) and lower for older people and long-term care (about 57–60%), so ages 19–64 are probably understated and 65 and over overstated. The shape is also from 2020, a pandemic year.
  • Veterans: VA publishes compensation and pension recipients and dollars in four age bands that differ from ours; they are spread within each band by Census population and scaled to the budget. VA health care by age was not found, so it follows the age mix of compensation recipients (a proxy).
  • Education: elementary and secondary dollars are spread over ages 5–18 and higher education and student aid over ages 18–29, both by population (a proxy; Pell grants and loan costs are not split by age, and the higher-education line may include student-loan accounting costs, so it is the least certain row).
  • Food aid: SNAP costs (USDA Food and Nutrition Service, FY2024) are spread by SNAP participants by age (FY2023 quality-control sample: children, adults 18–35, 36–59, 60 and older), assuming equal benefits per person. School meals, other child nutrition and WIC are counted at ages 0–17, so the mothers' share of WIC is counted with children. These FNS costs are not exactly the budget's food-assistance line, which is larger.
  • Housing aid: HUD's Picture of Subsidized Households (2024, all programs) gives units, average monthly federal spending and the age of the household head. Dollars are counted at the head's age, not spread over everyone in the household, so children in assisted housing are not counted as recipients; this is a different convention from SNAP and is labelled as such. HUD's site only serves browsers, so the file was saved with a browser session and is passed to the fetch script.
  • Income support: Supplemental Security Income uses the SSA SSI Annual Statistical Report 2025: federal SSI payments for calendar 2024 (about $60 billion, not a budget line, and a month's payment can fall in the neighbouring fiscal year, so it may run a few billion above fiscal-year cash; state supplements are state money and left out) are divided across under 18, 18–64 and 65 and older by the payments in December 2025 (the latest report's age split), then within the first two groups by recipients by finer age (under 5, 5–12, 13–17; 18–21 up to 60–64) and population; people 65 and older are spread over 65–100 by population. Unemployment insurance takes the budget line (OMB subfunction 603) and spreads it like the insured unemployed by age (Labor Department ETA 203, one count a month from October 2023 to September 2024, eight age groups), assuming equal benefits per person; the budget line also includes federal-employee and military claims and state administration grants, which get the same age mix; those 65 and older are spread over 65–74. SSA only serves browsers, so the SSI workbook was saved with a browser session and is passed to the fetch script.
  • Tax credits: IRS Statistics of Income tax year 2022 (table 2.5 for the earned income credit, table 3.3 for the child credits) give credits claimed. They are put at the likely age of the parent, not the child: each child's age plus the mother's age at birth (CDC final births, 2024, by age of mother), for children 0–16 (child tax credit) or 0–18 (EITC), weighted by Census population. Fathers and other claimants are typically a few years older and are not modelled, and today's birth ages are applied to all children. The childless EITC (about 4% of the credit) is spread over ages 25–64 by population. These are tax-year dollars and mostly reduce taxes owed rather than appear as spending, so they are shown in the chart but kept out of the share of outlays counted.
  • Two coverage figures: the page counts a share of all federal outlays, and a second share of only the outlays that pay for or serve people: Social Security, Medicare, health, income security, veterans, and education, training and social services (Treasury functions). Defense, interest on the debt, justice, transportation, science, energy, farm and commerce programs, international affairs, general government and offsetting receipts are mostly collective costs, not payments to individuals, and are left out of that second figure. Which functions count as “people” is a judgement: the health function, for example, includes research and public health.
  • Not allocated: defense, interest, federal employee retirement, ACA premium subsidies, the rest of food aid and the rest. Where there is no sound age split, the program is left out rather than guessed, so every figure is a floor. The page shows both coverage figures.
  • Population is the Census Bureau's July 2024 estimate by single year of age. Nothing nets out taxes paid, and nothing here says anyone deserved or benefited from any amount.

Who receives California state dollars (by age)

The same idea for the State of California's own money in fiscal year 2023–24, with the same five age brackets and generation labels as the federal page so the two can be compared.

  • By who spends, no double counting: only state funds are counted (General Fund, special funds and selected bond funds, as Schedule 9 of the Governor's Budget classifies them), including what the state sends to school districts and counties. Federal funds that pass through the same departments are left out because the federal page counts them; reimbursements between departments are left out too. Local money (such as the property-tax share of school funding) is not state money and is not here. Schedule 9 counts some fees, provider taxes, drug rebates and a few federally derived funds as state special funds; the page follows it.
  • Dollars are 2023–24 actuals from the 2025–26 Governor's Budget: Schedule 9 for each department, and the Department of Social Services budget page for its programs by fund (CalWORKs, child care, SSI/SSP, in-home care). The build checks that those programs add up to the department's Schedule 9 figure.
  • Schools and higher education are population proxies: K-12 over ages 4–18, UC, CSU and Cal Grants over 18–29, community colleges over 18–39. No enrolment by age was used, so older students are undercounted.
  • Medi-Cal is the whole Department of Health Care Services (almost all Medi-Cal), split in three steps. First, California's Medicaid benefit spending by eligibility group (MACPAC MACStats exhibit 21, federal fiscal year 2023: children 11%, expansion adults 30%, other adults 12%, disabled 25%, aged 23%). Second, the share the state pays: assumed 10% for expansion adults and 50% for every other group (temporary pandemic increases are ignored). The MACPAC figures cover federally matched benefits only: children covered through CHIP are not in them, and neither is coverage the state pays for entirely by itself, such as full Medi-Cal for adults without legal immigration status, which is mostly people aged 19–64. Their dollars are spread like the rest, so working-age adults are probably understated and children and older people overstated. Third, age: children at 0–18; adults by Medi-Cal eligibles aged 19–44 and 45–64 (DHCS, 2023–24 average); disabled people by SSI recipients' ages; aged people between 65–84 and 85 and over by CMS's national 2020 shape. It is still an estimate: the matching rates are assumptions, special funds are treated like the General Fund, and the state's payment to Medicare for older people's drug coverage is not separated out. The page does not treat the order of the youngest and oldest brackets as settled.
  • Disability and care: in-home supportive services follow recipients in six age groups (January 2024), assuming equal hours per person. Developmental services follow the department's regional-center spending for ages 0–2, 3–21 and 22 and over (annual purchase-of-service report, 2023–24: 6%, 18% and 76%), then the people served by finer age group within each. That report covers all funds, including federal reimbursements that go mostly to adults, and leaves out regional-center operations and state-run facilities, so it is a proxy for the state's own dollars.
  • Income support: the SSI state supplement follows California's SSI recipients under 18, 18–64 and 65 and over (SSA, December 2025). CalWORKs follows children and adults in cash-grant cases (months with a suppressed cell are skipped), with children placed at ages 0–17 and adults at 18–49.
  • Child care is spread over ages 0–12 by population.
  • Not allocated, listed on the page with dollars and reasons: prisons (the people held are not recipients in this page's sense), retirement contributions for teachers and state retirees (pay for past work), a school reserve withdrawal paid to schools and community colleges (the split between them is not in these files), realignment money and shared revenues passed to counties and cities, the rest of health and human services and education where no age split was found, and everything else (transportation, courts, natural resources, general government).
  • Population is the Census Bureau's July 2024 estimate for California by single year of age (civilian population; ages 85 and over are one group), so brackets with many service members are slightly undercounted.

Where school money goes

General-fund spending of the 42 school districts in San Diego County and the County Office of Education in 2023–24.

  • Source: California Department of Education, SACS unaudited actual data for 2023–24, the year-end ledgers every district files in the state's standard account codes. The state publishes one Microsoft Access database for all of California; the fetch step exports it with mdbtools and keeps only San Diego County's rows, the district list and the code titles. These are unaudited figures as filed.
  • What counts: the general fund (fund 01), object codes 1000–7499, which are expenditures. Transfers between a district's own funds are left out. Spending in every other fund (building, bond repayment, cafeteria, child development, adult education, self-insurance and so on) is listed separately and not mixed in.
  • Categories follow the state's object codes: certificated salaries (teachers 1100, pupil support 1200, administrators 1300, other 1900), classified salaries (2100–2900), benefits (CalSTRS 3100s, CalPERS 3200s, Social Security and Medicare 3300s, health and welfare 3400s, unemployment and workers' compensation 3500s–3600s, retiree health 3700s, other), books and supplies (4000s), services (5000s), capital outlay (6000s) and other outgo (7000–7499). Every expenditure row falls in exactly one category or the build fails.
  • State-paid pensions: the state makes part of the CalSTRS contribution on districts' behalf. Districts record it as both revenue and spending under resource 7690; the page shows it as its own line so it can be told apart from what districts pay.
  • Purpose uses the state's function codes (instruction 1000, special education instruction 1100s, school administration 2700, pupil services 3000s, general administration 7000s, plant services 8000s).
  • Revenue is general-fund revenue by object code: state aid under the funding formula and the Education Protection Account (8010s), local property taxes (8020–8089), property taxes passed to charter schools (8090s, negative), federal (8100–8299), other state (8300–8599), other local (8600–8699) and money passed on by other school agencies (8700s, mostly state special-education funding that arrives through the County Office of Education). The other 8090s are property-tax and formula transfers between funds and agencies. Revenue and spending differ in any one year.
  • Per student divides by the state's K-12 average daily attendance for the district. That count leaves out charter students whose school files its own accounts, and the County Office of Education has no count, so the county-wide per-student figures leave its spending out (it is about 5% of the total). The figure includes capital outlay and payments to other agencies, so it is higher than the state's published current expense per student.
  • Left out: rows that carry a school code are charter schools filing their own accounts under their authorizing district; they are kept out of the district's figures. The Special Education Pass-Through Fund is left out of "other funds" because districts count the same money as their own revenue and spending. Tuition and shared services paid between districts and the County Office (roughly 1–2% of the total) are counted by both sides, so the county total is slightly overstated.
  • Limits: the ledger has dollars, not employees, so it cannot show average pay or headcount. Districts differ in size, grade span and the share of students in special education, so per-student differences are not a measure of how well a district is run.

Checks that run on every build

  • Aggregating never changes a total.
  • Adopted budget stays within 25% of actual spending for every year that has both.
  • No year's total changes by more than 40% from the year before (this would catch double counting).
  • City comparison: every configured city must be found exactly once, every spending code must be mapped, and a city's total (leaving out schools, health and welfare) must be between $500 and $8,000 per resident.
  • Homelessness: the San Diego count must match in HUD's file and the state's file, San Diego must appear in every column of every HHAP table, every configured region must have a count in each counted year, and any unparseable value in the state file stops the build.

Margin estimates (spending compared with private-sector prices) are not on the site yet.
