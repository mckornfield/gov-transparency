Data & methods

Every number on this site comes from public files committed to the project's repository (data/raw/, with the source URL, retrieval date and checksum of each file in data/raw/MANIFEST.csv) and is rebuilt by scripts in scripts/.

Sources

  • Operating actuals (FY2011-23) and operating budget (FY2011-26): City of San Diego Department of Finance, data.sandiego.gov. Account and department reference tables come from the same portal.
  • Population: City of San Diego estimated population as reported in the California State Controller's Office local-government finance data (bythenumbers.sco.ca.gov).

How the numbers are built

  • Spending is every row whose account number starts with 5 (expense accounts). Revenue rows (starting with 4) are not used on this page.
  • Fiscal years are named by the year they end: FY2023 is July 2022 to June 2023.
  • Dollars are nominal, not adjusted for inflation.
  • Adopted budget only. The budget file also contains "proposed" rows for FY2024 and FY2025; they are excluded because adding them to the adopted rows would count the budget twice.
  • FY2026 budget rows have no budget stage in the source file. They are treated as the adopted budget. The City's open-data actuals file currently stops at FY2023.
  • Transfers Out (money moved between city funds) is about 13% of FY2023 spending. It is hidden by default because it counts the same dollars twice; the checkbox adds it back.
  • Credits. A department and spending type that nets to a credit cannot be drawn in a treemap. It is left out of the picture, included in the totals, and listed under the chart.
  • Unclassified. Rows whose department or account is missing from the City's reference tables are kept and labelled "Unclassified". Rows with a blank account number cannot be identified as spending or revenue and are excluded; their totals are recorded in data/processed/build_report.json.
  • Population is missing from the source for a few years. Those years are interpolated, and years after the latest report use the latest value. Per-resident figures for them are marked "(population estimated)".

Who gets paid (vendor payments)

  • Source: the City's yearly "Cumulative Amounts Paid to Vendors - $25,000 or more" reports (PDF), FY2022 to FY2026, read with pdftotext. The FY2026 report covers July 2025 to June 2026.
  • Only payments of $25,000 or more to a vendor in the year are published, so the totals leave out smaller vendors. They are not directly comparable to the City's spending either: spending includes payroll, while the vendor list includes capital, pass-through and other payments. The page shows the City's figure only for scale and says so.
  • No purpose or category is published, so a large payee can be a pass-through (for example to the pension system or a regional water wholesaler). The page makes no judgement about whether a payment was too high.
  • One company, several IDs or spellings. Vendors whose names match ignoring case, punctuation, "&" vs "and" and endings like Inc, LLC, Co or "The" are combined, as is a vendor ID renamed to another name starting with the same word. All vendor IDs are kept in data/processed/vendor_payments.csv. Two different companies with near-identical names could be combined by mistake.
  • Checks: the extractor fails on any line it does not recognise, page footers that are not exactly 1 to N, a page count that differs from the PDF's real page count, a duplicate vendor ID or an amount under $25,000. The reports print no totals, so the build also requires the number of vendors not to jump more than 35% year over year and each year's vendor payments to be between 40% and 110% of the City's own spending. The last check is a coarse sanity band (it mainly catches a lost first page or a double count); the page-count checks are what guard against a lost page.

Compared with other cities

  • Source: Census Bureau, Annual Survey of State and Local Government Finances, individual unit files for survey years 2019 to 2024 (census.gov), retrieved 2026-10-31. The 2022 survey was the five-yearly census of all governments. Amounts are in thousands of dollars in the source; the processed table stores dollars.
  • Peers: Phoenix, San Jose, San Antonio, Dallas, Austin, Fort Worth, Charlotte, Columbus, Seattle, Portland and Boston: US cities of roughly 0.6 to 1.7 million people, spread across regions. Seattle, Portland and Boston (0.64 to 0.75 million) are smaller than San Diego (1.38 million) and were added for regional spread. Consolidated city-counties (Indianapolis, Jacksonville, Philadelphia, San Francisco) are left out because they also provide county services. The list is in config/peers.json.
  • Services: each service is current-operation plus construction spending under the Census item codes listed in config/census_functions.json. The build fails if a spending code is not mapped. Utilities, transit and airports, "health, welfare, jails and schools", interest and grants are separate groups, off by default, because cities differ in whether they provide them. San Diego reports a small amount in the health and welfare group (about $94 per resident); Boston's city government runs its schools, so its figure there is much larger.
  • $0 means "not reported": usually a county or special district provides the service. Such cities are left out of the middle peer and the rank.
  • Some figures are Census estimates, not reports. Austin did not return the survey in most years: nearly all its lines in 2019 to 2022 and 2024 are flagged by the Census as imputed (estimated). Treat Austin's numbers as approximate. A few lines for other cities are flagged the same way.
  • Administration is a catch-all. It includes the Census's "general, not elsewhere classified" spending, so differences there may partly reflect how each city reports rather than how much it spends.
  • Fiscal-year timing: a survey year covers each government's fiscal year that ended between July of the year before and June of that year. In the 2024 survey San Diego's ended June 2024, but Dallas, San Antonio, Austin and Fort Worth ended September 2023 and Columbus and Seattle ended December 2023, up to nine months earlier. Nothing is adjusted for that timing.
  • Price adjustment (optional): with "Adjust for local prices" on, each city's per-resident figure is divided by its metro area's price level, the BEA Regional Price Parity for all items (US average = 100; apps.bea.gov, table MARPP, retrieved 2026-10-31). A city's fiscal year spans two calendar years, so the level is the two years blended by months (a year ended June 2024 is half 2023 and half 2024). Dallas and Fort Worth share one metro area; every other city has its own. The metro for each city is in config/peers.json. This is a rough correction: the index measures what households pay for goods and rent, not the wages and construction costs a city pays. It is off by default and the as-reported and adjusted gaps are both shown in the trend table.
  • Over time: the trend compares San Diego with the middle peer in each survey year, in dollars of the day (no inflation adjustment), so the gap is meaningful but the amounts are not comparable across years. To keep the years comparable, "other capital outlay" (equipment and land), which the 2019 to 2021 files list apart from construction and later files fold into it, is counted with construction in every year, and intergovernmental transfers, which older files list by service, are grouped into "Grants to other governments". Many cities' broad administration line jumps in the 2022 survey, so the single-service trends are steadier. Census population estimates change between years, and some cities carry the same estimate across two surveys.
  • Peer gap, not margin: peers share the same pressures and habits, so this says nothing about what a private-sector provider would charge.

Checks that run on every build

  • Aggregating never changes a total.
  • Adopted budget stays within 25% of actual spending for every year that has both.
  • No year's total changes by more than 40% from the year before (this would catch double counting).
  • City comparison: every configured city must be found exactly once, every spending code must be mapped, and a city's total (leaving out schools, health and welfare) must be between $500 and $8,000 per resident.

Margin estimates (spending compared with private-sector prices) are not on the site yet.
